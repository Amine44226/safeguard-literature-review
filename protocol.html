5.1 Step 1: search

Every search contains an addiction term, so no study can enter the review without one. The addiction terms are combined with the terms of one research question, which gives four searches.

Addiction terms: "digital addiction" OR "smartphone addiction" OR "smartphone dependence" OR "problematic smartphone use".

Searches A, B and C were run three times on each source: on the title, on the abstract and on the author keywords. These fields do not return the same studies. Search D was run on titles only. Its title run returned 1,278 results; an abstract run would have multiplied that number without adding studies the other searches miss. SpringerLink offers only a title field, so all its searches are title searches. PubMed has no abstract-only field: its abstract run uses title and abstract together.

Every run is recorded with the string as executed, the field, the filters, the date and the number of results the source reported.

5.2 Step 2: duplicates

A study found by several sources, searches or fields is kept once. Duplicates are found in three passes: an exact match on the DOI; then an exact match on the title with publication years no more than one apart; then a close match on the title, within the same year, for records that have no DOI. Two records that carry different DOIs are never merged.

5.3 Step 3: document type

Removed: papers not written in English (64), preprints (51), items that are not research papers such as editorials, letters and errata (49), books and book chapters (19), workshop and companion papers (3) and retracted papers (2).

5.4 Step 4: venue

A journal paper is kept if its journal is ranked Q1 or Q2 in the Scimago Journal Rank 2025 (best quartile, matched by ISSN, then by title). A conference paper is kept if its conference is ranked A*, A or B in the CORE Conference Ranking 2023. 388 studies in lower-ranked or unranked venues are removed. The 41 reviews found by the search are set aside here and used for the background of the review.

5.5 Step 5: title and abstract (filter layer 1)

Titles and abstracts are screened against filter layer 1. A study is included if it:

  • studies behaviour or mental state through smartphones or similar mobile devices;
  • answers at least one of the review questions;
  • reports original work, such as a study, a system, a dataset or an analysis;
  • was published in 2015 or later, in English, in a peer-reviewed venue;
  • has a full text that can be obtained.

A study is excluded if it only mentions the topic in passing, is an editorial, a conference abstract, a poster or a preprint, is centred on biological factors such as genes or molecules, or duplicates a study already included (the fullest version is kept). Each decision records the passage of the abstract it rests on. 136 studies are removed.

5.6 Step 6: selection per question (filter layer 2)

Filter layer 2 keeps, for each question, the studies that answer it most directly. A study is kept for a question if it:

  • RQ1: studies how smartphone addiction is measured in young people, and gives a new or revised scale, a cut-off, a definition, or a check against phone data;
  • RQ2: uses data recorded by the phone;
  • RQ3: measures human values in young people, or follows young people over time and tests the link with a mental health problem in both directions, or collects daily or momentary data on their mental health;
  • RQ4: uses machine learning or builds a system.

Young people are school and university students, including health-science students, adolescents, and young adults aged up to 29. A sample counts if it consists of school or university students or adolescents, or if its stated age range lies within 10 to 29. Three rules settle the other cases.

  • The rule is judged on the abstract. A sample that the abstract does not describe does not count. If only a mean age is reported, the described population decides.
  • In a study with several samples, one sample that meets the definition is enough.
  • Students count at any age.

93 studies are kept: 27 for RQ1, 16 for RQ2, 32 for RQ3 and 32 for RQ4. A study can be kept for more than one question.

Filter layer 2 was amended twice after full texts had been read and scored. First, the young-people rule was applied to every RQ1 study. Then a version of a scale in Arabic or Persian stopped being kept on that ground, and the definition of young people was widened to young adults aged up to 29 and applied to every study. The 93 studies include 6 that passed the layer as it stood when they were read and fail it as amended. They are removed at step 7.

5.7 Step 7: full text and quality

91 full texts are read and checked again against filter layer 1. Removed on reading: 6 that answer none of the questions, 2 conference abstracts, 1 preprint, 1 paper not in English, and 1 study whose results repeat those of another study. The remaining 80 are scored for quality (section 6).

Nine scored studies are then removed under criteria amended after scoring: the 6 that fail filter layer 2 as amended (section 5.6), and 3 in which smartphone addiction is only a predictor of another outcome. A study of that kind answers none of the questions. The rule was applied to every scored study.

Of the 71 studies left, 24 score below 3.5 and are removed; 47 are included.

Four other full texts were read before the step 5 decisions recorded here were adopted, and one of them was scored. These studies are excluded at step 5. In all, 95 full texts were read.

6. Quality assessment

Every study read in full is scored on six questions, taken from the quality checklist for quantitative studies of Kitchenham and Charters (2007). Each is answered yes (1), partly (0.5) or no (0), so totals run from 0 to 6.

  1. Are the aims clearly stated? Does the study state a clear objective for what it sets out to collect, model or predict?
  2. Do the study measures allow the questions to be answered? Did the study have the measures to connect its behavioural data to the smartphone addiction, human values or psychological disorder outcome it set out to study?
  3. Are the data collection methods adequately described? Does it say which data were captured, and how?
  4. Are the statistical methods justified? Does it explain and justify the statistical or AI approach it used?
  5. Were the basic data adequately described? Are the sample, variables and results actually shown, not only summarised?
  6. Do the researchers explain the consequences of any problems with the validity or reliability of their measures?

A written scoring guide fixes what yes, partly and no mean for each question and each study design: instrument validation, cross-sectional survey, longitudinal panel, daily diary, smartphone sensing, machine learning and qualitative studies.

When two answers could apply to a question, the lower one is kept.

Studies scoring 5 to 6 are rated high, 3.5 to 4.5 moderate, and below 3.5 low. Only moderate and high quality studies are included. The cut-off was 3 when the studies were scored. It was raised to 3.5 after scoring. The change removed 14 studies that scored 3, 6 of them RQ2 studies.

7. Data extraction

The following is recorded from each included study, one row per study.

  • Reference details: authors, year, title, venue.
  • Study aim and the questions it answers.
  • Context: country, participants, sample size and study length.
  • Design.
  • Data collected: behaviours, sensors and contextual signals captured.
  • Dataset or data source: the named dataset or data collection used, if any.
  • System and platform: architecture and data pipeline.
  • Methods: the statistical or AI techniques and their validation.
  • Smartphone addiction: how the study defines or measures it.
  • Outcomes: the psychological states, values, disorders or addiction constructs targeted.
  • Instruments: the validated questionnaires or scales used.
  • Performance: the results reported, with accuracy or effect measures.
  • Ethics and privacy: consent, anonymisation, data security and governance.
  • Quality score: the total from section 6.

8. Synthesis

The studies are summarised in tables and text, question by question. Where studies report comparable figures, these are put side by side. Where they do not, they are summarised in text. Findings from high quality studies carry more weight. Studies that draw on the same participants count as one source of evidence.
